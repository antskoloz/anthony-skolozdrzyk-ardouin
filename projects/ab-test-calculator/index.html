How to read your results

Plan a test tells you how many visitors each variant needs so that, if the lift you care about is real, you will detect it with the power you chose. Decide this number before you launch and stick to it.

Check my results compares two variants you already ran. The p-value is the chance of seeing a difference this large if the variants were truly identical; below your threshold (10%, 5% or 1% for 90, 95 or 99% confidence) the result is called statistically significant. The confidence interval is the range of lifts that are consistent with your data: it is often more useful than the p-value because it shows how big, or how small, the real effect could be.

Statistical significance is not the same as business significance: a tiny lift can be significant with enough traffic, and a large lift can be inconclusive with too little.

Methodology

Sample size per variant for a two-proportion test, where p₁ is the baseline rate, p₂ = p₁ plus the lift, and p̄ = (p₁ + p₂) / 2:

n = ( z(α)·√(2·p̄·(1−p̄)) + z(β)·√(p₁(1−p₁) + p₂(1−p₂)) )² / (p₂ − p₁)²

z(α) is the critical value for your confidence level (1.645 / 1.960 / 2.576 for 90 / 95 / 99% two-sided) and z(β) is the value for your power (0.842 for 80%). The result is rounded up.

Results check: the p-value comes from a two-sided two-proportion z-test using the pooled rate. The confidence interval is on the absolute difference p₂ − p₁ (Wald interval with the unpooled standard error); the relative interval divides its bounds by the control rate, which is an approximation. The "smallest detectable lift" is the lift your sample could detect at 80% power. Observed power is deliberately not reported: it is just another way of restating the p-value and is misleading.

Assumptions: each visitor is counted once, visitors are randomly assigned, the groups are independent, and the sample is large enough for the normal approximation (roughly 10 or more conversions and non-conversions in each group). This calculator does not correct for multiple variants, repeated looks at the data or sequential testing.

Good practice: fix your sample size in advance, do not stop at the first significant reading, run for at least one full business cycle (a week or more), and treat testing many variants or many metrics at once with extra caution.

Frequently asked questions

How many visitors do I need for an A/B test?

It depends on your baseline conversion rate, the smallest lift you care about, the confidence level and the statistical power. Lower baselines and smaller lifts need far more visitors. Enter those values in the Plan a test tab to get the required sample size per variant.

What does 95% confidence mean in an A/B test?

It means that if there were truly no difference between the variants, you would see a result this extreme less than 5% of the time. It does not mean there is a 95% probability that the variant is better.

What is statistical power?

Power is the probability that your test detects a real lift of at least the size you planned for. 80% is the common default: if the lift is real, you would detect it 8 times out of 10.

Can I stop my A/B test as soon as it reaches significance?

Not safely. Checking repeatedly and stopping at the first significant result inflates the false-positive rate well above your chosen level. Decide the sample size in advance and read the result once it is reached.

My result is not significant. Does that mean there is no difference?

No. It means the data do not provide enough evidence to tell the variants apart. Look at the confidence interval: a wide interval that includes zero usually means the test was too small, not that the variants are equal.
